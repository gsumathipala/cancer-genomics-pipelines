A profile is the settings one kit needs, named once. It chooses interval padding, the allele-fraction and depth floors, whether duplicate marking and base recalibration are meaningful for that chemistry, the UMI layout, and which report statistics the target is large enough to support. Picking one on the new-run form applies all of it; anything you fill in yourself still wins.

Profiles come in two kinds. A DNA profile configures comprehensive_variant_calling.py and needs your kit’s target BED. An RNA profile configures fusion_calling.py instead — a different pipeline, with STAR and Arriba rather than bwa-mem2 and Mutect2 — and needs a GENCODE annotation and a STAR index. The two forms (DNA, RNA) each offer only their own, and each refuses the other’s.

No profile ships a target BED. Vendor BEDs are licensed content that travels with the kit, they are specific to a kit version and a genome build, and the wrong one is worse than none — it looks like a target region and silently is not one. So every profile names the BED as something you supply.

Built-ins are a starting point, not an authority. They carry published figures for one version of each kit. Kits are revised, laboratories spike in extra content, and a validated limit of detection belongs to your validation rather than to a catalogue number. To add your own, write a JSON profile into ~/.config/cancer_pipeline/panels/ — one is written for you by python comprehensive_variant_calling.py --new-panel-template mine.json. A profile whose id matches a built-in replaces it.
